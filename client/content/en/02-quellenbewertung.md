A source is not automatically good just because it is official. Official recommendations are valuable starting points, but may be outdated, politically or administratively cautious, or not fully in line with current scientific consensus. What is crucial is timeliness, evidence base, transparency and consistency with multiple good sources.

## 2.1 Priority 1: Guidelines and official sources

Guidelines and official sources are the first anchor for general health recommendations, safety limits and public guidance. However, they should not be adopted blindly: publication date, methodological basis, target group and topicality remain crucial. Typical examples are DGE, Federal Ministry of Health, Gesund.bund.de, AWMF/DGSM and WHO.

## 2.2 Priority 2: scientific reviews and position papers

Reviews and position papers are particularly valuable when it comes to sports nutrition, training and supplements. However, their quality depends heavily on which studies were included, how current the evaluation is, whether conflicts of interest are disclosed and whether the results are practically transferable. Examples include ISSN, ACSM and PubMed-listed reviews.

## 2.3 Priority 3: Coaches and YouTube channels

Coaches and YouTube channels can provide practical classification, exercise execution, motivation and clear examples. They are most useful when they provide good sources and their recommendations fit guidelines, reviews and their own context. They are too weak as the sole primary source.

## 2.4 Priority 4: Influencer Content

Influencer content can provide ideas, entertainment and motivation. However, technical statements from this must always be checked against basic principles, guidelines and good studies, especially if the focus is on fear, promises of miracles, product sales or oversimplifications.

## 2.5 Test questions

- **Currentness:** Is the source new enough for the topic?
- **Evidence base:** Are studies, reviews or guidelines mentioned transparently?
- **Consensus:** Do multiple independent, high-quality sources roughly agree?
- **Target group:** Does the statement apply to healthy adults, athletes, sick people, children or older people?
- **Conflicts of interest:** Are there product sales, sponsorships, political agendas or strong brand loyalty?
- **Practical transferability:** Does the recommendation fit with everyday life, level of training, goal and risk?

## 2.6 Formulations

- **"Recommended"** means: supported by a current, comprehensible source and compatible with the rough expert consensus.
- **“Frequently useful”** means: good practice area, but individually adaptable.
- **“Check”** means: first look at trends, context and symptoms, not react to a single daily value.
