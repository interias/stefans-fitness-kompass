# 09 Biomechanics and Exercise Selection

<p align="center">
  <img src="../assets/visuals/fitness-kompass-biomechanik-uebungsauswahl-banner.png" alt="Fitness Compass Biomechanics Exercise Selection Banner" width="100%">
</p>

Biomechanics is not intended to replace an anatomy lexicon in the fitness compass. The practical benefit is simpler: understanding which movement strains which muscle, why an exercise is a good or bad fit for the goal and when an additional isolation exercise is really necessary.

The principle remains: first cover movement patterns cleanly, then optimize details. For most healthy adults, a robust exercise selection that regularly trains squat or leg press patterns, hinges, presses, pulls, core stability, and grip strength is sufficient. Individual muscles, angles and exercise sequences only become important when the goal, technique and repeatability are already correct.

## 9.1 Principle: Movement before muscle names

In everyday life, the body does not train isolated muscles, but movements. Muscles generate force, slow down movement and stabilize joints; Depending on the task, they work as main movers, supporters, stabilizers or opponents (<a href="https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/11-1-interactions-of-skeletal-muscles-their-fascicle-arrangement-and-their-lever-systems">OpenStax 2022a</a>). An exercise is not automatically good because it is colored in a muscle graphic. It is good if it reliably loads the target muscle, remains technically repeatable, can be increased over weeks and feels acceptable for the joints and everyday life.

Muscles rarely work alone. During a squat, the quadriceps, glutes, adductors, core and erector spinae work together. When rowing, the lats, middle back, rear shoulder, biceps, forearms and torso work to varying degrees depending on the variation. In practice, it is therefore more important to determine which movement pattern and which bottleneck should be trained than to reduce an exercise to a single muscle.

## 9.2 How muscles work in practice

A muscle pulls on bones via tendons and thereby moves one or more joints. If the muscle shortens, it works concentrically; if it brakes the movement under load, it works eccentrically; if he holds position, he works isometrically. All three forms occur in strength training and are regulated by the nervous system via motor units (<a href="https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-4-nervous-system-control-of-muscle-tension">OpenStax 2022b</a>).

The most important thing for building muscle is repeated mechanical tension in the stressed fibers. This is explained in more detail in [08 Strength Training](08-krafttraining.md). Biomechanics complements this logic: a muscle only receives a good stimulus if the exercise loads it in a position in which it actually has to contribute meaningful force. That's why small changes sometimes feel significantly different: grip width, elbow angle, upper body tilt, foot position or range of motion shift the lever and target muscles.

Anatomical details are useful, but not every person needs to know the origin, insertion, and every muscle head by heart. The question is often enough: Which joint is moving, in which direction is the load going, and which muscle has to generate or slow down this movement?

## 9.3 Compound exercises and isolation

Compound exercises move multiple joints and stress multiple muscle groups at the same time. Squats, deadlift variations, bench presses, shoulder presses, pull-ups, lat pull-downs, rows, lunges and carries are therefore time efficient. They provide a lot of training stimulus, but require more technique, coordination and regeneration.

Isolation exercises usually move a major joint and make it easier to target a target muscle. Lateral raises, leg curls, leg extensions, bicep curls, tricep presses, butterflies and calf raises are not "less functional" but tools. They are particularly useful when a muscle does not receive enough stimulation in compound exercises, when joints need to be protected or when an aesthetic goal is specifically prioritized. Exercise variation and exercise selection can influence strength and hypertrophy adaptations, but do not replace the basic variables of volume, intensity, technique and progression (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/">Kassiano et al. 2022</a>).

| Decision | Often useful | Border |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Little time, general fitness | Cover compound exercises and large movement patterns first | individual muscles may remain underrepresented |
| Build muscle specifically | Compound exercise plus appropriate isolation | not every fiber needs special exercises immediately |
| Joint or technique limited | Check machine, cable pull or more stable variant | Don't solve pain with more willpower |
| Advanced priority | Place target muscle earlier in the unit | other exercises lose performance |

## 9.4 Lower Body: Squat, Hinge and Lunge

The lower body can be practically understood through three major patterns: knee-dominant exercises, hip-dominant exercises, and single-leg or staggered variations. Knee-dominant exercises such as squats, leg presses or leg extensions primarily put strain on the quadriceps, i.e. the knee extension. Hip-dominant exercises like the Romanian deadlift, hip thrust, or good morning put more strain on the glutes and hamstrings. Lunges, split squats and step-ups connect both worlds and require additional stability.

| muscle group | Main function in training | Simple practice examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quadriceps | Straighten your knees and control your bending movements | Squat, leg press, hack squat, leg extension |
| Gluteus maximus | Extend your hips and stabilize your pelvis | Hip thrust, squat, split squat, Romanian deadlift |
| Hamstrings | Extend your hips and bend your knees | Romanian deadlift, leg curls, good morning |
| Adductors | Pull your leg in, stabilize your hips and pelvis | deep squat, leg press, adductor machine, cossack squat |
| Calves | Stretch the ankle, cast when walking and running | standing and seated calf raises, jumps, stairs |

Squats and leg presses are good basic tools, but they don't automatically replace all posterior chain work. Those who only train knee-dominantly often leave hamstrings and hip extension underexposed. Conversely, heavy deadlifts do not always replace adequate quadriceps work. A robust plan therefore usually contains at least one knee-dominant and one hip-dominant pattern.

## 9.5 Core: Stability, rotation and transfer

The torso is more than just visible abdominal muscles. Rectus abdominis, obliques, transversus abdominis, erector spinae, pelvic floor, diaphragm and deep stabilizing structures help control the pelvis, ribcage and spine under load. In everyday life and in sports, it is often not about moving the torso to the maximum, but rather about transferring power between the lower and upper body.

<p align="center">
  <img src="../assets/visuals/fitness-kompass-biomechanik-rumpf-muskeln.png" alt="Fitness-Kompass Biomechanik Rumpf Muskeln" width="100%">
</p>

*Image: The torso connects the ribcage, spine and pelvis. Visible abs are just part of it; Lateral and deep layers help control against movement.*

In practice, four tasks can be distinguished:

1. **Control flexion:** Crunches and cable crunches train more active trunk flexion.
2. **Prevent overextension:** Planks, dead bugs and ab wheel variations train anti-extension.
3. **Creating or braking rotation:** Pallof presses, carries and controlled rotations train anti-rotation and power transfer.
4. **Control side bends:** Side planks and one-sided carries train lateral stability.

Core training doesn't have to be extremely hard every day. When heavy squats, deadlifts, rows and carries occur regularly, the core is already doing a lot of postural work. Additional core work makes sense if stability, sport requirements, back feeling or target aesthetics speak for it. Recurring back or nerve pain is a warning sign and does not belong in an experiment with ever harder abdominal exercises.

## 9.6 Back: Pulling, rowing and shoulder blade control

Back training isn't just about "hitting the lats." The lats pull the upper arm down, back and towards the body; it is put under a lot of strain during pull-ups, lat pull-downs and some rowing movements. The middle back with rhomboids and middle trapezius moves and stabilizes the shoulder blades, especially during rowing movements. The upper trapezius helps with scapular elevation and rotation; the back shoulder works hard in wide rows, reverse flies, and face pull variations.

<p align="center">
  <img src="../assets/visuals/fitness-kompass-biomechanik-ruecken-zugmuskeln.png" alt="Fitness-Kompass Biomechanik Ruecken Zugmuskeln" width="100%">
</p>

*Image: Latissimus, trapezius, middle back and rear shoulder lie around the shoulder blade, spine and upper arm. Shift the pull angle and shoulder blade movement, which part works particularly hard.*

Vertical pulls like pull-ups or lat pull-downs are an easy way to regularly load the lats. Rowing complements this with horizontal pulling and more work on the middle back, rear shoulder and scapular control. The closer the elbows stay to the body, the more lat-focused rowing often feels. The wider the elbows are, the more the back shoulder, upper back and trapezius come to the fore.

“Pulling your shoulders back” isn’t the only thing that counts when it comes to posture and shoulder feeling. It makes more sense to have a strong, flexible shoulder girdle that can pull, push, lift and rotate. If your neck, shoulder or elbows regularly become irritated, first check your grip, range of motion, load, volume and exercise variant.

## 9.7 Chest: Pressing and adduction

The pectoralis major moves the upper arm in front of the body and towards the center of the body. Pressing exercises such as bench presses, dumbbell presses, push-ups and dips stress the chest along with the front shoulder and triceps. Fly variations such as butterfly or cable flies reduce the triceps component and make horizontal adduction more directly noticeable.

<p align="center">
  <img src="../assets/visuals/fitness-kompass-biomechanik-pectoralis-fasern.png" alt="Fitness-Kompass Biomechanik Pectoralis Fasern" width="100%">
</p>

*Illustration: The pectoralis major extends from the collarbone, sternum and upper ribs to the upper arm. The grain pattern explains why upper arm angle, bench inclination and fly variations can shift the stimulus.*

Incline bench presses often shift the stimulus more towards the upper chest and front shoulder. Flat bench, light incline bench, dumbbell variations and machines can all work if the range of motion, control and progression are right. Dips can be more chest-focused if the upper body is tilted slightly forward and the elbows are not extremely tight; More upright variations shift the stimulus more towards the triceps and shoulders. Shoulder feeling remains the limit here.

The order of butterfly before incline bench or incline bench before butterfly is not a question of faith. Incline bench first fits when load, progression and technical quality are priorities in the main lift. Butterfly first can be useful if you want to feel the chest better or if you want to work with lower pressure loads; In return, the performance usually decreases during subsequent pressing. What matters is what delivers a better target stimulus that can be reproduced over several weeks.

## 9.8 Shoulder: front, side and back parts

The deltoid has broadly three practical roles. The front shoulder works hard during pressing movements such as the bench press, incline press, and shoulder press. The lateral shoulder raises the arm to the side and is less fully covered by many compound exercises. The back shoulder pulls the upper arm back and out and works in rows, reverse flies and face pulls.

<p align="center">
  <img src="../assets/visuals/fitness-kompass-biomechanik-schulter-deltoideus.png" alt="Fitness-Kompass Biomechanik Schulter Deltoideus" width="100%">
</p>

*Image: The deltoid surrounds the shoulder joint like a cap. Anterior, lateral and posterior fibers pull on the upper arm from different directions and therefore respond to different exercise angles.*

For many plans, the front shoulder is already sufficiently or even heavily loaded. The side shoulder, on the other hand, often needs direct work if shoulder width or appearance is a goal. Lateral raises with dumbbells, cables or machines are usually easier than doing more and more shoulder presses. The rear shoulder and upper back benefit from rowing and reverse fly variations, especially when there is a lot of pressing.

The rotator cuff stabilizes the humeral head in the shoulder joint. It doesn't have to be trained with lots of special exercises in every plan, but proper shoulder control, appropriate exercise variations and measured volume are important. Stinging shoulder pain, instability or radiating discomfort are warning signs.

## 9.9 Arms, forearms and grip strength

Arms are already trained when pushing and pulling, but direct work can be useful. The biceps flexes the elbow and externally rotates the forearm. The brachialis lies under the biceps and also flexes the elbow; Hammer curls or neutral grip variations often hit him well. The triceps extends the elbow; The long head also crosses the shoulder joint and is subjected to greater strain in the stretched position in overhead versions.

<p align="center">
  <img src="../assets/visuals/fitness-kompass-biomechanik-arme-griffkraft.png" alt="Fitness-Kompass Biomechanik Arme Griffkraft" width="100%">
</p>

*Image: Upper arm, forearm and hand work together to pull, push and grip. Grip strength depends not only on the hand, but also on the forearm muscles, tendon path and elbow position.*

Forearms and grip strength are not just looks. They often limit pulls, carries, climbing, hanging, OCR and Spartan-like requirements. Farmer carries, suitcase carries, dead hangs, pull-up variations, thicker grips and controlled curls can train grip strength and forearms for everyday use.

Direct arm work must match the overall volume. Anyone who pulls and pushes a lot and also does a lot of curls and triceps exercises often notices overload first in their elbows or forearms. Then the grip, range of motion, frequency and set proximity to failure must be checked.

## 9.10 Neck and throat muscles

The neck and cervical muscles stabilize the head, cervical spine and shoulder girdle. The upper trapezius works in shrugs, carries, heavy lifting and many pulling movements. Specific neck muscle training can be relevant in certain sports, such as motor sports, martial arts or contact sports, because head and neck control are more required there.

<p align="center">
  <img src="../assets/visuals/fitness-kompass-biomechanik-nacken-hals.png" alt="Fitness-Kompass Biomechanik Nacken Hals" width="100%">
</p>

*Figure: Neck muscles, upper trapezius, clavicle, shoulder girdle and cervical spine form a stability system. This area is important for general fitness, but rarely an early primary lever.*

For general fitness, direct neck building is rarely an early key lever. A strong upper back, good shoulder control, carries and sensible pulling exercises cover a lot. If you specifically train the neck, you should start conservatively: small ranges of motion, low loads, slow progression and no experiments if you experience dizziness, radiating pain, numbness or headaches.

## 9.11 Efficiency: 80-90% with few good exercises

The efficiency approach does not mean that details are irrelevant. It means that most progress comes from a few repeatable basic decisions. A plan that neatly covers major movement patterns, contains sufficiently hard sets, and is continued over months is stronger for most goals than a constantly changing specialized plan.

| Movement patterns | Covers roughly | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Squat/leg press | Quadriceps, glutes, adductors, core | Squat, leg press, hack squat |
| Hinge | Hamstrings, glutes, back extensors | Romanian deadlift, hip thrust, good morning |
| Horizontal pressing | Chest, front shoulder, triceps | Bench press, dumbbell press, push-up |
| Vertical or horizontal dragging | Latissimus, upper back, rear shoulder, biceps | Pull-ups, lat pull-downs, rows |
| Shoulder isolation | lateral and posterior shoulder | Lateral Raise, Reverse Fly, Face Pull |
| Core/Carry/Grip | Core control, grip strength, transmission | Pallof Press, Farmer Carry, Dead Hang |

A simple full-body plan can cover a lot: leg press or squat, Romanian deadlift or leg curl, bench press or push-up, lat pull-up or pull-up, row, lateral raise, a core or carry variation. If you can only do two to three strength training sessions per week, you should first stabilize this coverage. Special exercises come next when a target muscle lags behind despite a clean basic structure.

## 9.12 Exercise sequence, pre-fatigue and priorities

The order in a session controls attention, performance and fatigue. Technically demanding, difficult or prioritized exercises usually belong earlier in the unit; Reviews on exercise order practically advocate sorting exercises by training goal and priority (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/">Nunes et al. 2020</a>). Isolation exercises, pump work, drop sets, or short blocks of supersets often work better later, when the main work is done.

Pre-fatigue can be a tool, but it is not a mandatory principle. The literature is heterogeneous and does not demonstrate a simple rule that pre-fatigue generally produces better strength or hypertrophy adaptations (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9022698/">Trindade et al. 2022</a>). If you do butterfly before pressing, you can feel your chest more and work with lower pressing loads. At the same time, performance in the subsequent compound exercise often drops. Those who press first can follow the load and progression better, but they don't always feel the chest as clearly. Both variants can make sense; the context decides.

The same goes for supersets: you'll save time if the exercises aren't too competitive. Cable rows plus dumbbell presses or lateral raises plus triceps presses are often easier to control than two exercises that directly fatigue the same muscle. For difficult technique, maximum strength or new movements, more rest is usually the better decision (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39903375/">Zhang et al. 2025</a>).
